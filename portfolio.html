01 · Pipeline

Pipeline

Rebuilding the screen mortgage advisors live in all day — from a legacy monolith to a micro-service product used nationwide.

RoleSole designer for 2.5 yrs, end to end
Duration4 years on the domain · launched Oct 2025
UsersIn-house advisors & external brokers
TeamCross-functional squad, product + engineering
Context

Interhyp ran its advisory business on one large legacy system. Over the years it became expensive to maintain and impossible to extend — it could not carry newer technology, and its interface patterns were far behind what the business needed. In 2021 the company decided to break it apart into domains, each served by its own micro-services.

The Pipeline sits in the Workflow domain: the working surface where an advisor sees every case they're responsible for, and decides what to do next. It's the screen they open first in the morning and keep open all day.

My role

I joined the early Pipeline work as a junior designer, and have been the domain's sole designer for the past two and a half years — owning research, concept, interaction design, testing and every release since.

Approach
  • Rebuilt the Pipeline from the ground up rather than porting the old screens, so the new information architecture could follow how advisors actually triage work.
  • Ran 5 moderated usability studies with more than 30 participants across two genuinely different audiences: Interhyp's in-house advisors, and external brokers whose daily routine and incentives differ.
  • Built and tested multiple prototype rounds before development committed to a direction.
  • Took part in a weekly feedback and testing loop with product managers and users for four years, and turned what came out of it into a continuous release stream.
Key decision

Coverage (Vertretung). Before a holiday, an advisor had to hand over their open to-dos by reassigning each one to a colleague by hand — the kind of task that gets rushed or skipped the day before leaving, and quietly costs the team afterwards. I designed a coverage concept that does the whole handover automatically in three clicks.

Shipped

Advanced filtering and sorting · coverage · advanced settings · continuous visual and interaction improvements, released iteratively rather than as one big drop.

Outcome
5 wavesStaged nationwide rollout, Oct 2025 – Mar 2026, reaching tens of thousands of brokers
~65%Growth in active professional users in the first three months after launch
30+Research participants across five moderated studies

Qualitative response to the redesign has been consistently strong through the weekly product and user sessions.

02 · Videoberatung

Videoberatung

Replacing a licensed video tool with an in-house product — designed, built and shipped in one week.

RoleOwned design end to end
DurationOne-week build
UsersAdvisors and their clients
Team7 · product owners, designers, engineers
Context

Advisors ran video consultations through a licensed third-party tool. The licence was a flat annual fee — the company paid the same amount whether the tool was used heavily or barely at all. Interhyp decided to bring video advisory in-house, inside the Interaction domain that carries every channel between advisor, client and lender.

This was one of the first projects I owned. Time pressure was extreme: the entire product was developed in a single week.

Approach
  • Ran workshops to establish what advisors actually needed from a consultation call, rather than copying the incumbent tool's feature set.
  • Shaped the concept through moderated testing on prototypes — even inside a one-week window, the concept was validated before it was built.
  • Built a dedicated design system for the product, since it was new and had no existing patterns to inherit.
Outcome
~80%Reduction in annual video-advisory spend, and a shift from a flat licence to usage-based cost
~50%Month-on-month growth in consultations at the start of 2026
86%Of consultations run with the camera on — the behaviour the design set out to encourage

The camera-on rate is the number I care about most. A video advisory tool people use with the camera off has failed at the thing it exists for; the design goal was to make turning the camera on the natural default, and it held as usage scaled.

03 · Monetisation & Licence Management

Monetisation & licence management

A B2B storefront where external brokers buy add-on services for the Home platform — designed from scratch, in a domain with no precedent.

RoleSole designer, from scratch
DomainAPI Governance & Monetisation
UsersExternal brokers
ConditionsTight deadlines, unfamiliar problem space
Context

Interhyp's Home platform can be extended with additional services and extensions that external brokers buy for their own business. That requires two things that didn't exist: a way to browse and purchase, and a way to manage the licences afterwards — which tier a broker holds, what it unlocks, when it renews.

It was my first e-commerce work and my first exposure to licence management, and the brief arrived ambiguous and on a deadline.

Approach
  • Ran stakeholder and user feedback workshops to convert a vague commercial ambition into a defined problem before designing anything.
  • Used AI-assisted prototyping (Figma Make) to get from brief to testable artefact fast — the constraint was time, so the tooling had to remove time from the front of the process, not the end.
  • Designed both halves as one experience: buying and then living with what you bought.

07 · Master's thesis case study · Interhyp Group

A collaboration model for the Interhyp Home platform

How should a mortgage advisor and their client work on the same application, at the same time, without stepping on each other's changes? I spent five months researching, designing and testing an answer — and it became my Master's thesis, graded 1.0.

My roleUX design, research & testing (solo, with a UX team supervisor)
Timeline5 months · 2022
CompanyInterhyp Group, Munich
ToolsAdobe XD, Miro, Microsoft Teams
Background

One platform, replacing three

Interhyp was merging three separate applications — Home, Sales and Credit — into a single platform, Interhyp Home, used by mortgage advisors, bank employees, clients and agents together. For the first time, these groups would overlap on the same screens.

The company had three goals for the new collaborative features: make every action in the platform transparent to everyone involved, keep the home-buying process simple, and keep the platform scalable as more user groups were added.

To keep a five-month thesis realistic, my supervisor and I narrowed the scope to two roles — the agent (a freelance mortgage broker) and the client — and to one step in their shared workflow: data entry, where both sides fill in and verify the same application data. It's the point with the most back-and-forth, and the most to lose if it's confusing.

Research question: How does an optimal collaboration model between the agent and the client look within the Interhyp Home platform?

Approach

Design thinking, in five months

Interhyp's stakeholders had fixed requirements and milestones; my university needed a rigorous, documented process. Design thinking satisfied both — it's iterative enough to revisit earlier steps when testing turns up something new, which is exactly what happened here.

Empathize

What "good collaboration" already looks like

I started with a competitive analysis across four kinds of collaborative tools — creative boards (Miro), documents (Google Docs), project management (Trello) and communication (Microsoft Teams, Slack) — to see how each handled the same underlying problem.

Across all four, five recurring patterns stood out as the features worth adapting: history / versions, notifications or activity feed, real-time collaboration, online / offline status, and comments. These became the candidate feature set for everything that followed.

Personas & context of use

Agents turned out to be less uniform than the initial brief assumed, so I built two agent personas and validated them with Prohyp's customer service team, who speak with agents daily.

Florian Schneider

Financial advisor · primary persona

Full-time, tech-comfortable, handles a high volume of applications and wants speed above all.

Volker Schmidt

Real estate agent · secondary persona

Financing is a side task next to his main business; needs the platform to ask little of him.

Susanne Freundlich

Client

First-time buyer, relies heavily on her agent and wants to feel informed, not overwhelmed.

Alexander Kluge

Client

More experienced with paperwork, wants control and a clear record of who changed what.

40%only about 40% of agents actually matched the "Florian" persona — yet, according to Interhyp's customer service team, that group generates 60–70% of Interhyp's revenue. Designing for their speed and volume mattered more than designing for the average case.

Both rows tell the same story: satisfaction holds steady through the early consultation, then drops sharply the moment one side discovers the other changed something — "I did that?! But that's wrong, I have to correct that!" — with no way to see what changed, when, or why. That single moment became the design target for the rest of the project.

Define

Six candidate features, one problem statement

Combining the competitive research with the journey map gave me six features to evaluate: activity history, notifications, real-time collaboration, online/offline status, comments, and version history.

The problem I defined from this: agents and clients edit the same data asynchronously, with no shared record of what changed, who changed it, or whether the other person is even active right now — and that gap is what turns a routine correction into a moment of frustration and lost trust.

Ideate & Prototype

From paper to a clickable prototype

I sketched each feature on paper first, then built clickable prototypes in Adobe XD on top of Interhyp Home's existing UI — the interface itself was already defined, so my job was to design how these collaborative layers sat on it.

Test

Two rounds of testing, one real pivot

"Prototype as if you know you're right, but test as if you know you're wrong" — I took that literally and ran two rounds.

Round 1 — Internal expert review

4 participants: two UX experts, one developer, one product stakeholder. Caught structural issues early and got the design aligned with company requirements before it reached real users.

Round 2 — External usability test

5 participants from Prohyp customer service, who speak with agents daily and were asked to role-play as one. Remote, moderated, think-aloud sessions over Microsoft Teams with a clickable Adobe XD prototype — I observed rather than facilitated, so I could focus entirely on reactions, hesitations and mouse movement.

Results

What the usability test actually found

I scored every issue on frequency, task impact, business impact and persistence, following Nielsen's severity model, then prioritized fixes with my supervisor and the stakeholders.

Evaluation

Assumption → observation → design change

The value of testing wasn't confirming what worked — it was the handful of moments where the data disagreed with my assumptions.

Activity history & notifications

Assumed: an activity feed would be self-explanatory and its icon instantly recognizable.
Observed: every participant understood the feed once they found it — but none found the icon without help.
Changed: gave the icon stronger visual weight and treated discoverability as its own design problem, separate from the feed's content.

Version history

Assumed: version history and activity history were different enough to stay as two features.
Observed: participants kept expecting them in the same place and were confused they weren't.
Changed: merged both into one panel with two tabs, and reordered version entries to lead with what changed rather than the date.

Comments

Assumed: attaching comments directly to a data field would make communication clearer for both sides.
Observed: confirmed — participants immediately understood and used the feature correctly.
Changed: kept the core interaction, and added archiving to the roadmap after multiple participants asked for it unprompted.

Real-time & online/offline status

Assumed: seeing when the other person was online, at all times, would build trust.
Observed: agents juggling many clients found always-visible status distracting rather than reassuring.
Changed: made the indicator something a user could choose to hide, rather than a fixed always-on signal.
Outcome

Recommendation & reflection

Based on the testing, I recommended Interhyp implement all five collaborative features for Interhyp Home's data-entry step, with the specific refinements above — the combined history panel, the more discoverable icon, adjustable presence, and comment archiving on the backlog. The thesis was graded 1.0.

If I ran it again, I'd test with real agents rather than customer-service staff role-playing as them, and I'd run a third round focused specifically on the two problems I suspected were caused by how I worded the tasks, not the design itself.

What stayed with me most: the biggest usability win here wasn't a clever interaction, it was making sure the loudest, most frequent pain point — "wait, what changed and why?" — had one obvious place to get answered.
